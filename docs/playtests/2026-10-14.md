## What the evidence says

The old run target was 220 px/s, but the measured steady speed on a flat 0.8-friction floor was 118.565 px/s.
The movement controller steers toward its target while floor friction and damping remove speed each tick.
Changing data at that existing controller fixes the demonstrated slow response without replacing contact physics.

| Direct 360-px target input-to-hit time, seconds | 0.100 | miss | 0.200 | 0.250 |

A uses ground control .35 and leaves braking .02. B uses .50 control and .10 air control but also leaves braking .02.
Both make running faster but introduce excessive stop travel. C first combined B's response with .20 braking and A's 1300/.60 shot settings.
The selected refinement uses .12 braking and .40 projectile gravity so direct shots hit the practice target and moving-platform riding remains useful.
The final build also uses projectile density .0013846154, preserving the former speed-times-mass terrain kick while reducing travel speed.
Fixed card/mechanic tests now declare their lab trajectory and movement rather than taking mutable product defaults as fixed inputs.
The shipped-data practice and loose-piece tests, plus the replay comparisons, remain separate checks of the selected defaults.
The block, jump, damage, magazine, reload, recoil and global-gravity values stay at their previous settings.

A 100-px/s moving-platform trial confirmed the consultation's predicted braking risk:
.20 braking loses 25.677 pixels relative to the platform over one second; .12 loses 3.433 pixels, with the fighter remaining grounded.
The old .02 braking has effectively zero drift. This is a recorded tradeoff, not a claim of identical carrying behavior.
The selected .12 setting keeps stop travel under one third of a 44-pixel body width and avoids the severe .20 slipping.
Direct shots at 480 and 640 pixels still require aiming above the target; the game retains projectile arcs.
